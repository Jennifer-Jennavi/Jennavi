Best LinkedIn Positioning Books for Founders in 2026

Positioning gets used to describe two completely different problems, and most founders get pointed to the wrong book for the one they actually have. A fair comparison of what each LinkedIn positioning book actually solves, and which one you need first.
By Jennifer Mmesoma Omaliko ◆ July 13, 2026 ◆ 10 min read ◆ Jennavi

A founder searches "best LinkedIn positioning book" and gets handed a stack of titles that are not actually solving the same problem.

Some of them are about a strategic question: who is your product for, and why does that specific person care. Some of them are about a tactical one: how does that answer actually show up in a profile, a post, a DM, so a stranger scrolling LinkedIn recognises it and acts on it. Nobody making the recommendation usually says which kind you are getting.

That distinction is the entire reason this guide exists. Positioning is not one problem wearing different book covers. It is two different problems, and reading the wrong resource first wastes months.

Positioning is two different problems, not one

Market positioning is the strategic layer. It asks who your product is genuinely for, what makes it different from the obvious alternatives, and why that difference matters enough for someone to choose you. This work happens before a single LinkedIn post gets written. Get it wrong and no amount of clever content fixes it, because you would be broadcasting a story about a product that has not decided who it is.

LinkedIn positioning is the execution layer. It asks how that market position actually gets communicated through a headline, an About section, a content mix, and a DM, so a stranger with no context recognises themselves in it within seconds and takes a next step. This is a completely different skill from the strategic one, and a founder can be excellent at one while being invisible because of the other.

Most "best positioning book" lists blend both categories together without saying so. That is why the recommendations feel scattered. They are not competing answers to the same question. They are answers to two different questions that happen to share a word.
